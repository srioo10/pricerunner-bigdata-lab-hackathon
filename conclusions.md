# Conclusions

## Descriptive analytics
- The raw file was already tidy: no missing values and no exact duplicate rows. Most of the noise sits inside the titles. Cleaning removed 852 of 35,311 listings (2.41%): 41 titles with no informative word and 811 titles longer than the 1.5xIQR fence of 105 characters.
- Two cleaning mistakes showed up only in later steps and were fixed. German listings had lost their umlauts ('geschirrsp ler'), which produced a nonsense per-category rule {geschirrsp} -> {ler}; 1,987 listings were repaired and translated. The model-code filter also deleted spec words such as 1400rpm and lga1151 (2,359 occurrences), so it now keeps them.
- Selection kept the title words and the category. Product ID is an identifier and Category ID is a copy of the category. Cluster Label is the product name, and its words are already in the titles. Cluster ID is kept only to build a test split with unseen products.
- The 10 categories are mildly imbalanced (Fridge Freezers 5,408 vs Freezers 2,195, ratio 2.46). Title length is close to symmetric. Listings per product are right-skewed (skew 1.73), and Box-Cox brings this down to 0.005. Brand (the first informative word of the title, used as a proxy) and token counts are heavy-tailed: 25 first words cover 70% of listings, and token frequency falls off with a Zipf slope of -1.53 (fitted over ranks 100 to 4,354).

## Predictive analytics
- Apriori at 1% support and 80% confidence finds 1,129 frequent itemsets and 3,528 rules. 466 of these rules have a category as the consequent. They describe which words go with which category, but they were mined on all listings, so they are not a tested classifier. Brand-model pairs have the highest lift ({apple} -> {iphone}, lift 74.8). Dropping support from 5% to 1% makes Apriori about 4.4 times slower (0.94 s to 4.13 s at 80% confidence). Without the category item the same settings give 570 itemsets and 992 word-to-word rules. Inside single categories, 5-10% support finds model rules that the global run misses, such as {hobbs, microwave} -> {russell}.
- The decision tree reaches 94.8% test accuracy and 0.949 macro F1, and 94.0% on products it never saw in training. Its settings and vocabulary size were picked by 5-fold cross-validation on the training rows only. Chi-square selection did not help: cross-validation kept all 1,972 words. The tree beats both Naive Bayes baselines on the same features (92.7% and 93.1%). Cleaning does not change accuracy (raw titles give 94.7%), but the cleaned tree has 216 fewer leaves. Most errors are Fridges labelled as Fridge Freezers (66 test listings).
- The tuned tree has 1,206 leaves, so its IF-THEN rules are many and long. For TVs, dishwashers, CPUs, microwaves, washing machines and fridge freezers a rule of 3 to 6 conditions is enough: one product word (tv, dishwasher, intel, microwave) plus a few words that must be absent. The fridge and freezer categories need 15 or more conditions, which matches where the tree makes its errors.
- K-Means on TF-IDF/LSA vectors finds no natural number of clusters: silhouette stays between 0.046 and 0.161 for k from 2 to 24. k=10 was used to match the number of categories. Over 5 seeds NMI against the categories is 0.707 +- 0.041 (range 0.649 to 0.762). The seed-42 run shown in Section 6 is the lowest of the five, with purity 0.668. Six clusters are at least 95% one category (microwaves, mobile phones, CPUs, washing machines, digital cameras, TVs). The largest cluster (7,342 listings) is the most mixed: dishwashers, mobile phones, washing machines and fridges, held together by shared words such as integrated, white and place.
- In the dendrogram of category centroids, Freezers and Fridge Freezers merge first (0.326) and CPUs merge last (0.878). The decision tree's most common error (Fridges labelled as Fridge Freezers) is inside this same refrigeration group.
- MAFIA finds 156 maximal frequent itemsets at 1% support, against 699 closed and 1,129 frequent ones. mlxtend's fpmax returns exactly the same 156 sets. MAFIA took 0.038 s and fpmax 0.71 s, but this compares compiled C++ with Python, not the two algorithms. The longest maximal itemsets have 7 items and describe a whole product type, e.g. {4k, hd, hdr, smart, tv, ultra, cat=TVs}.

## PySpark (local multicore scaling)
- Spark gives the same results as the single-machine libraries. FPGrowth finds the same 1,129 itemsets with the same counts, and Spark's decision tree scores 0.868 against sklearn's 0.869 on the same features.
- On the data as given, Spark is slower in every test (speedups 0.41, 0.27 and 0.55). Each Spark job has a fixed start cost that a 34k-row file cannot pay back.
- With the data copied up to 20 times, Spark's time grows much more slowly. The decision tree breaks even at 2x (0.98) and is 2.26 times faster at 5x, 3.36 at 10x and 3.95 at 20x. FPGrowth on Spark beats mlxtend Apriori only at 10x (1.44). Against mlxtend's own FP-growth it only draws level at 20x (1.00), so the gain for association rules comes mostly from the algorithm, not from Spark.
- The Spark tree scores lower (0.868) than the tuned tree in Section 5 (0.948). The benchmark uses a fixed 1,000-word vocabulary and depth 20 on both sides so the two engines get the same task.

## Limitations
- All timings come from one 16-core machine in Spark local mode against single-threaded Python. They show local multicore scaling, not a cluster.
- The larger inputs are copies of the same data. This measures throughput only; it says nothing about how the models would do on new products.
